Old English and Anglo-Saxon Names

This list has two kinds of name. The first are real Anglo-Saxon names, recorded for people who lived in England before 1066: Æthelflæd, Eadgifu, Wulfstan, Leofric. The second are names still used today that grew out of them, such as Audrey (from Æthelthryth), Edith, Alfred and Edwin, plus a few modern names made from Old English place names.

Most Anglo-Saxon names were built from two ordinary words, so the meanings here are given as their parts: Eadgifu is ead (prosperity) plus gifu (gift). We checked them against Behind the Name, the Oxford Dictionary of First Names and the Prosopography of Anglo-Saxon England.

Why the meanings are given as parts

An Anglo-Saxon name was usually two elements joined together, chosen partly from the family's stock of names. Æthelwulf of Wessex named his sons Æthelstan, Æthelbald, Æthelberht, Æthelred and Ælfred. The result did not have to make sense as a phrase, so reading Wulfstan as "wolf-stone" tells you what the parts mean, not what the parents meant. That is why this page writes most meanings as "wolf + stone" rather than turning them into a sentence.

The second element also usually showed whether a name was a woman's or a man's. Names ending in -gifu, -gyth, -thryth, -burh, -flæd and -wynn are women's; names ending in -stan, -weard, -ric and -wine are men's.

Spellings: æ, th and burh

Old English had letters modern English dropped. Æ was a vowel like the a in cat, and the sound th was written with þ or ð, which we give as th. You will see the same name spelled several ways: Æthelthryth, Æðelþryð and Ethelthryth are one name. Some women's names end in -burh or -burg, which are two spellings of the same word for a fortress.

If you use one of these names for a child, the simplest route is a modern descendant (Audrey, Edith, Alfred) or a plain spelling such as Ethelfled or Wulfric. For a historical story, keeping the æ looks right on the page.

Which names survived

After the Norman Conquest most English families moved to names like William, Robert and Alice within a few generations. The Old English names that lasted mostly had a saint or a royal behind them: Edward the Confessor, Edmund the martyr, Audrey of Ely. Many others survived only as surnames, such as Goodwin from Godwine and Lewin from Leofwine.

The Victorians brought a batch back, including Alfred, Edgar, Edwin, Edith, Hilda and Mildred, and invented Ethel as a short form of Æthel- names. Modern names like Ashley, Shirley, Stanley and Bradley are Old English too, but at one remove: they began as place names, became surnames and only then became first names.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What are some Anglo-Saxon names?
Recorded examples include Æthelstan, Ælfred, Eadweard, Leofric, Wulfstan and Godwine for men, and Æthelflæd, Eadgifu, Eadgyth, Hild and Wulfrun for women. Each was used by people who lived in England before 1066.
+Are any Anglo-Saxon names still used today?
Yes. Edward, Edmund, Alfred, Edwin, Edgar, Oswald and Harold for boys, and Audrey, Edith and Hilda for girls, all come from Old English names. Alfred, Edward, Edith and Audrey are in regular use again in England and Wales.
+What do Old English names mean?
Most are two words joined: Alfred is elf + counsel, Edith is prosperity + war, Mildred is gentle + strength. The parts were chosen by family custom and naming rules, so the meaning is a list of parts rather than a description of the person.
