How the Plane Name Generator Works

It works from 1,863 names and name parts, which combine into over 33,000 possible results.

The choices:

  • Style: Any style, Elemental, Heavenly, Infernal, Fey and shadow, Astral/dream, Chaotic.
  • Name format:

    • With a description, e.g. Halura, where the souls of the good rest in endless gardens

Each press gives you 8 names. "Show 8 more" adds 8 you haven't seen yet. Once you've seen them all, "Start again" shuffles the whole list again.

Good plane names, in the D&D sense of planes of existence rather than aeroplanes, describe what the place is made of or what it's for: The Brine Ocean for an elemental plane, The Ivory Terraces for a heaven, The Tiers of Sealed Fates for a hell. This plane name generator names D&D planes of existence and other dimensions: elemental planes, heavens, hells, fey and shadow realms, astral and dream planes, and planes of pure chaos, with a short description if you want one.

Eight good plane names

All eight came from the generator, across its six styles:

  • The Twelve Floating Mountains: an earth and air plane described in four words.
  • The Ivory Terraces, where the souls of the good rest in endless gardens: a heaven built in levels, which gives the party somewhere to climb.
  • The Tiers of the Tally, where the air smells of sulphur and old parchment: a hell run on paperwork.
  • The Glimmering Courts, where travellers who eat the food can never leave: a fey plane with the oldest fairy rule of all.
  • Palehollow, a grey echo of the world where colours drain away and sounds grow faint: a shadow plane in a single word.
  • The Drift of Waking Dreams, a starry void crossed by ships with silver sails: an astral plane for travelling through.
  • Skrelurra, where gravity is a suggestion rather than a rule: a chaos plane with harsh sounds to match.

Planes in D&D and before it

The idea of an astral plane is older than the game. It came from 19th-century occultism and Theosophy, which described layers of existence beyond the physical world. D&D took the word and built a whole cosmology on it.

  • The Inner Planes are the elements: fire, water, air and earth, and the places where they meet.
  • The Outer Planes are the realms of gods and the afterlife, from the heavens to the hells. Earlier editions arranged them in a ring known as the Great Wheel.
  • The Feywild and the Shadowfell are the 5th edition's echoes of the mortal world: one brighter and wilder, the other grey and drained of life.
  • The Astral Plane is the silvery space between them, crossed by travellers and their ships.

The generator follows the same divisions, so its names slot into a D&D cosmology or your own. Choose With a description for a line about what's there.

Building your own cosmology

  • Name planes after what they're made of or who lives there. Players remember "the hell of contracts" better than an invented word.
  • Use a short name and a long one: Caldira in conversation, Plane of Boiling Seas on the map.
  • Give each plane one rule that makes it different, like not eating the food, or gravity that changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What are some good D&D plane names?
Names that describe the plane, like The Brine Ocean for an elemental plane, The Ivory Terraces for a heaven, or Palehollow for a shadow realm. Choose a style for elemental, heavenly, infernal, fey, astral or chaotic planes.
+What is a plane of existence?
In D&D and other fantasy, a separate world or dimension beyond the ordinary one: the elemental planes, the heavens and hells, the realm of the fey, or the starry Astral Plane between them.
+Are these the official D&D planes?
No. They're new names in the same style, so you can add them to an existing D&D cosmology or build your own without borrowing from a published setting.
