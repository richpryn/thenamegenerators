Trolls in folklore

Trolls come from Scandinavian folklore, where they live in mountains, forests and under the hills, and hate the light.

  • Turned to stone: in many Norwegian tales a troll caught by the sunrise turns to stone, which is one explanation for the odd shapes of some rocks.
  • The bridge troll comes from "The Three Billy Goats Gruff", a Norwegian folk tale collected by Peter Christen Asbjørnsen and Jørgen Moe in the 1840s.
  • The Dovre King rules the trolls of the Dovre mountains in Henrik Ibsen's play Peer Gynt (1867), and the painter Theodor Kittelsen fixed how trolls look for many Norwegians.
  • In D&D, trolls regenerate, and only fire or acid stops them.

Naming a troll

  • Folklore trolls get Norse-sounding names, often with a home "of the Dovre Mountains".
  • Cave trolls get names made of what they're covered in: Mossback, Bouldernose.
  • D&D trolls get short, harsh names like Vorrgar.
  • Bridge trolls get a name and an address: Wartle Stonesitter, Clod of the Humpback Bridge.

Frequently Asked Questions

+Do trolls turn to stone in sunlight?
In many Scandinavian tales, yes: a troll caught out at sunrise turns to stone.
+Where does the bridge troll come from?
From the Norwegian folk tale The Three Billy Goats Gruff, collected by Asbjørnsen and Moe in the 1840s.
+What's a good name for a troll?
A Norse-sounding name for a folklore troll, a craggy one like Mossback for a cave troll, or a name and an address like Grumble Underbridge for a bridge troll.
