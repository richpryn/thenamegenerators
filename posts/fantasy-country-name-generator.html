How real countries got their names

Most real country names come from one of four places, and fantasy names can use the same ones:

  • A people. England is the land of the Angles; France is named after the Franks. Denmark is "the march of the Danes", mark meaning a border land.
  • The land itself. The Netherlands are the low lands, Montenegro the black mountain, Iceland just what it says.
  • Something precious. Argentina comes from the Latin argentum, silver, which explorers hoped to find there.
  • A person. Bolivia is named after Simón Bolívar, the Philippines after Philip II of Spain.

Tolkien worked the same way in Elvish: Gondor means "stone-land" and Mordor "black land".

Six styles

  • Elvish and flowing: soft sounds and endings like -dor (land), -lond (haven) and -wen. Celanor, Silwen.
  • Northern and Norse: endings from the Scandinavian languages. -heim is home or world (Norse myth has Jotunheim and Vanaheim), -gard an enclosure (Midgard, Asgard), -rike a realm, -vik a bay. Skarvik, Ravnholm.
  • Imperial and Latin: Roman names ended in -ia (Gallia, Britannia, Hispania), and these follow suit. Severica, Aurenna, Corvium.
  • Old English kingdoms: before England, there were Wessex, Mercia and Northumbria. These use Anglo-Saxon name parts like aelf (elf), wulf and beorn (warrior). Aelfwold, Wulfmarch.
  • Dark and dangerous: hard consonants and growling endings for the enemy across the mountains. Drakgrim, Nekoth, Malkhal.
  • Islands and the sea: island words like -holm and -ey, and -ennis, which echoes the Welsh ynys and Cornish enys, both "island". Gwynholm, Tolennis.

Kingdom, empire or republic?

Choose "With a title" and the name comes with a form of government, which says a lot about the place:

  • Kingdom and High Kingdom: one crown, or a king over other kings.
  • Empire and Imperium: rule over other peoples, usually won by conquest.
  • Grand Duchy and Principality: smaller states, often squeezed between bigger ones.
  • Marches: border country, held by lords whose job is to keep the neighbours out.
  • Jarldoms and Thanes: a land of chieftains rather than a single throne.
  • Most Serene Republic: the title of Venice, a merchant state run by its rich families.
  • Thalassocracy: from the Greek for "sea power", a realm whose strength is its fleet rather than its land.

Fitting a country onto your map

  • Make neighbours sound different. A Norse-sounding kingdom next to a Latin empire tells the reader at once that these are two peoples, and probably old enemies.
  • Match the towns. A country's towns should share its sound. Use the Fantasy Town Name Generator for English-style towns, and keep a few syllables from the country name for its capital.
  • Give it another name. Countries rarely call themselves what their neighbours call them: Germany is Deutschland to Germans and Allemagne to the French. One country with two names is cheap and very convincing worldbuilding.
  • Say the adjective. You'll write about its people more than the country, so check how the name sounds with -ian, -ish or -er on the end before you settle on it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are these names from Tolkien or Game of Thrones?

No. They're built from sounds in the style of each language, and famous fictional and real country names that the parts happen to make are left out. Middle-earth is a model for the elvish style, but the names are new.

What's the difference between the styles?

Each uses the sounds of a different language: flowing elvish names, Norse names ending in -heim and -gard, Latin names ending in -ia and -ium, Old English kingdoms like Mercia, harsh names for dark lands, and island names ending in -holm and -ey.

Can I use it as a kingdom name generator?

Yes. Choose "With a title" to get kingdoms, empires, grand duchies and more, or use a name on its own and add your own title. The style decides how the kingdom sounds; the title decides how it is ruled.

How do I name the people of a fantasy country?

Add an ending and say it aloud: -ian (Severican), -er (Valdheimer), -ish (Aelfwoldish) or -i (Nekothi). Pick whichever is easiest to say, and use it consistently.

Can I use these names in D&D or my book?

Yes, they're free to use and you don't need to credit the site.
