Real pirates and their nicknames

The Golden Age of Piracy, roughly the 1650s to the 1730s, gave us most of the pirates in popular memory, and their nicknames usually came from how they looked or dressed:

  • Blackbeard, Edward Teach (or Thatch), for his huge black beard.
  • Calico Jack, John Rackham, for his brightly coloured calico clothes.
  • Black Bart, Bartholomew Roberts, one of the most successful pirates of the age.
  • Black Sam, Samuel Bellamy, captain of the Whydah.

Women sailed as pirates too: Anne Bonny and Mary Read served with Calico Jack's crew.

Naming a pirate ship

Real pirate ships were often captured and renamed by their new owners, and the names could be grand or defiant: Blackbeard's Queen Anne's Revenge, Bartholomew Roberts' Royal Fortune, William Kidd's Adventure Galley. A "Revenge" was a popular choice. The generator's ship names mix the classic patterns: an adjective and a creature, a Revenge, or a ship named after someone.

Making your pirate memorable

  • Give the nickname a reason. Every good pirate nickname has a story: how did Peg-Leg lose the leg?
  • Match the name to the role. A captain needs a name the crew will fear; a ship's cook can be Salty Ned.
  • Use it for fun: fancy dress, a pirate party, Talk Like a Pirate Day on 19 September, or a tabletop campaign on the high seas.

Frequently Asked Questions

How did pirates get their nicknames?

Usually from their looks or clothes: Blackbeard's beard, Calico Jack's calico clothes. Some came from their deeds or their reputation.

Were there real female pirates?

Yes. Anne Bonny and Mary Read sailed with Calico Jack Rackham in the early 18th century.

What are some famous pirate ship names?

Queen Anne's Revenge (Blackbeard), Royal Fortune (Bartholomew Roberts), Whydah (Samuel Bellamy) and Adventure Galley (William Kidd).
