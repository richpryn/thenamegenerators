How the French Name Generator Works

It works from 2,302 names and name parts, which combine into over 620,000 possible results.

The choices:

  • Gender: Any, Male, Female.
  • Era: Any era, Popular now, Born 1950–1979, Born 1900–1939.
  • Name format:
    • Full name, e.g. Adrienne Laurent
    • First name only, e.g. Manon
    • Surname only, e.g. Delorme

Where the names come from: These are real recorded names. Every first name and surname is real. First names are the most given names in each era in INSEE's Fichier des prénoms (édition 2025), which counts the names of babies born in France from 1900 to 2025. Surnames are the most common among people born in France whose deaths INSEE recorded in 2023, 2024 and 2025 (Fichiers des personnes décédées). Both come from INSEE and are reused under the Licence Ouverte 2.0. A full name joins a first name and a surname at random, so the pairing is new. Left out: surnames that are brands (Renault, Lacoste), that read as English insults, or that are better known as first names, and a few names whose accents the records don't show. Pairings that match a famous person, such as Antoine Dupont or Claude François, are blocked.

The labels on each name: Real parts a new combination of real names or name parts, made by this generator. Our Naming Library explains how we check names.

Each press gives you 8 names. "Show 8 more" adds 8 you haven't seen yet. Once you've seen them all, "Start again" shuffles the whole list again.

French names follow the decades: Marcel and Jeanne were born before the Second World War, Philippe and Nathalie in the 1960s, Léo and Jade in the last few years. This French name generator uses INSEE's official records of the names given to babies born in France, so every first name and surname is one that real people have. Pick an era to match a character's age, or get a full name, a first name or a surname on its own.

Eight good French names

  • Léa Martin: Léa has been one of the most given girls' names of recent years, and Martin is the most common surname in the list.
  • Raphaël Lambert: a boy's name near the very top of the 2016–2025 records, with the diaeresis that tells you to say Ra-pha-ël.
  • Anaïs Le Gall: Le Gall is a Breton surname; the Le stays a separate word with a capital L.
  • Augustin Fournier: an old name back in fashion, with a trade surname (a fournier worked the oven).
  • Nathalie Mercier: Nathalie was among the most given girls' names of 1950–1979; a mercier sold cloth and small wares.
  • Jean-Baptiste Leroy: a compound first name with a surname that means 'the king'.
  • Marie-Claire Dupont: Dupont means 'of the bridge', and it's one of France's best-known surnames.
  • Marcel Lefèvre: Marcel was one of the most given boys' names of 1900–1939, and fèvre is an old French word for a smith.

How French names work

First names come in two kinds: single names like Louise and Gabriel, and compound names joined with a hyphen, like Jean-Pierre, Marie-Claire and Jean-Baptiste. A compound name counts as one first name, not two.

Until 1993 parents in France had to choose from a limited stock of names: a law of 1803 (11 Germinal an XI) allowed names from calendars of saints and figures of ancient history. The law of 8 January 1993 gave parents a free choice, though a registrar can report a name to the public prosecutor if it seems against the child's interest. Recent lists include names like Maëlys, Lyna and Timéo alongside Louise and Jules.

Surnames mostly grew from a handful of sources:

  • First names of an ancestor: Martin, Bernard, Thomas, Robert, Richard.
  • Trades: Lefèvre and Lefebvre (smith), Meunier (miller), Charpentier (carpenter), Mercier.
  • Places and landscape: Dubois ('of the wood'), Fontaine ('fountain'), Dupont.
  • Nicknames: Petit ('small'), Leroy ('the king'), Leblanc ('the white').
  • Breton names with a separate Le: Le Gall, Le Goff, Le Roux.

Since 2005 parents can give a child the father's surname, the mother's, or both in the order they choose. This generator gives one surname, the way most French people's names still look.

Which era should you pick?

A French name says a lot about when someone was born, so match the era to your character's age:

  • Popular now (2016–2025): short, soft names. Gabriel, Léo, Raphaël, Louis and Jules for boys; Louise, Jade, Emma, Alice and Ambre for girls. Right for children and teenagers.
  • Born 1950–1979: the parents and grandparents of today's children. Philippe, Patrick, Alain and Christophe; Nathalie, Isabelle, Sylvie and Catherine.
  • Born 1900–1939: Jean, André, Pierre, Marcel and René; Marie, Jeanne, Madeleine and Suzanne. Good for a story set in the 1940s or 50s, or an elderly character today.

Some old names have come back, which is why Marcel, Augustin and Léon turn up in the recent lists too.

Older records were often typed in capitals without accents, so a few names appear as they were registered: Emile, Etienne and Eric are far more common in the records than Émile, Étienne and Éric. Where the records show an accented spelling often enough, the generator uses it.

Using a French name for a character

  • Keep the accents. Most girls called Léa or Inès in INSEE's records is written with the accent, and a missing one is the first thing a French reader notices.
  • Mind the region. Le Gall and Le Goff are Breton; Muller, Meyer and Schmitt are common in Alsace and Lorraine; Payet, Hoarau and Grondin are names of Réunion.
  • Check it isn't someone famous. Pairings of real names can match a real person. The generator blocks the famous ones it knows, but search the name before you publish.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What are the most common French surnames?
Martin, Bernard, Thomas, Petit, Durand, Robert and Dubois top the list, counted from INSEE's records. Choose Surname only to see more.
+What are popular French girl names?
In recent years Louise, Jade, Emma, Alice and Ambre have been among the most given. Choose Female and Popular now (2016–2025).
+What are old-fashioned French names?
Marcel, René, Roger and Maurice for boys, Jeanne, Madeleine, Suzanne and Yvonne for girls. Choose Born 1900–1939.
+Are these real French names?
Yes. Every first name and surname comes from INSEE, France's national statistics office. The generator joins them at random, so a full name is a new pairing rather than a real person.
+Why do some French names have a hyphen?
Compound first names such as Jean-Pierre, Marie-Claire and Jean-Baptiste are written with a hyphen and count as one name. They were most common for children born from the 1940s to the 1960s, so choose Born 1950–1979 to see plenty.
+Can I use a French name from this generator for a character?
Yes, the names are free to use. Pairings that match famous people are blocked, but it's worth searching a full name before you publish a book or game.
