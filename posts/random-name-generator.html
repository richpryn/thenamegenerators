How the Random Name Generator Works

It works from 12,549 names and name parts, which combine into over 6,200,000 possible results.

The choices:

  • Country: UK or USA, UK, USA.
  • Gender: Any, Female, Male.
  • Era: Any era, Recent, 2000s and 2010s, 1990s, 1900s to 1980s.
  • What you get:
    • Full name, e.g. Alexandra Thompson
    • First name only, e.g. Chloe
    • Surname only, e.g. Moody

Where the names come from: These are real historical names. Every first name and surname is a real one from official records. UK first names come from the Office for National Statistics (ONS) baby names for England and Wales, 1996 to 2025, and its top 100 lists every ten years from 1904; UK surnames from the ONS counts of surnames on birth registrations in England and Wales in 2025 (both under the Open Government Licence v3.0). US first names come from the Social Security Administration's baby names, 1880 to 2017 (public domain, via the babynames data package), and US surnames from the US Census Bureau's 2010 surname list (public domain). A full name joins a real first name to a real surname, so the pair itself is new. Every recorded name that makes the cut is kept, even ones that are also everyday words. Surnames that are women's names or titles in the register, such as Begum, Kaur and Bibi, only follow a girl's first name, and well-known full names (famous people and characters) are left out.

The labels on each name: Real name a real name, found in historical records or in real use. Real parts a new combination of real names or name parts, made by this generator. Our Naming Library explains how we check names.

Each press gives you 8 names. "Show 8 more" adds 8 you haven't seen yet. Once you've seen them all, "Start again" shuffles the whole list again.

A random name is most useful when it sounds like a real person: the kind of name you'd see on a class register or a staff rota. This random name generator picks real first names and surnames from official records in the UK and the USA, by gender and by the era a name was popular, as a full name, a first name or a surname. It gives names only: no addresses, birthdays or made-up identity details.

Where the names come from

Every name is in an official record, so it's one real people have:

  • UK first names come from the Office for National Statistics (ONS), which counts the first names on every birth registered in England and Wales. Recent uses the top names of 2020 and 2025, 2000s and 2010s those of 2005, 2010 and 2015, 1990s those of 1994 and 1996, and 1900s to 1980s the ONS top 100 lists for every tenth year from 1904 to 1984.
  • UK surnames come from the ONS count of surnames on birth registrations in England and Wales in 2025, so they're the names British families have now. Smith, Khan, Ahmed, Ali and Jones were the top five.
  • US first names come from the Social Security Administration, which counts the names on applications for Social Security cards. The copy used here runs to 2017, so Recent means 2015 to 2017 in the USA.
  • US surnames come from the US Census Bureau's list of the commonest surnames in the 2010 Census, led by Smith, Johnson and Williams.

The ONS data is published under the Open Government Licence; the US lists are public domain. A full name is a new pairing of a real first name and a real surname, which is why it shows the Real parts badge. Well-known full names, from famous people to fictional characters, are left out.

Matching a name to a character's age

First names date people, and the fashion changes fast. In England and Wales the top names were Susan and David in 1964, Sophie and Jack in 1996, and Olivia and Muhammad in 2025. In the US they were Linda and James in 1950, Jessica and Michael in 1990, and Emma and Liam in 2017.

So pick the era from when your character was born, not when the story is set. A head teacher in her fifties suits 1900s to 1980s, a new graduate 2000s and 2010s, and a toddler called Margaret will sound like a family name passed down. Surnames don't date in the same way, so the era only changes the first name.

For invented names in fantasy, sci-fi or comic styles, try the Character Name Generator, and for a surname with its meaning, the Last Name Generator.

Using random names for testing and placeholders

  • Test the awkward ones. Real names include apostrophes (O'Brien), capitals in the middle (McKenzie), hyphens (Isla-Rose) and spaces (De La Cruz). A form or database that can't take them will turn real customers away.
  • Keep it to names. The generator gives names only. If you need test addresses or phone numbers, use ones set aside for testing, not real ones.
  • Remember someone has it. These are real names, and a random full name may belong to a real person. Use them for characters, game players, sample data and placeholders, never to pass yourself off as someone else.
  • Mix the countries. Choose UK or USA and Any era for a spread of names that looks like a real customer list.

Frequently Asked Questions

+How do I get a random first and last name?
Leave What you get on Full name and press the button. Each name is a real first name with a real surname from the same country, such as Freya Robertson or Logan Rivera.
+Are these real names?
Yes. Every first name and surname comes from official records: the ONS for England and Wales, and the Social Security Administration and Census Bureau for the USA. The full names are new pairings, so they're unlikely to belong to anyone famous.
+Can I get a random British or English name?
Choose UK. The first names are ones registered for babies in England and Wales, and the surnames are those of families registering births there in 2025.
+How do I get a random boy or girl name?
Choose Male or Female under Gender, and First name only if you just want the first name. Add an era to get a name that fits the character's age.
+Is this a fake name generator?
It makes names, nothing else: no addresses, birthdays or ID numbers. Use them for stories, games, test data and placeholders, not to deceive anyone.
+Why do some names sound old-fashioned?
Because they were popular a long time ago. Choose Recent for names given to babies now, or 1900s to 1980s for names like Margaret and Linda that suit older characters.
+Can I use these names in my book or game?
Yes, they're free to use. Well-known full names are left out, but search a name before you give it to a major character, in case a real person shares it.
