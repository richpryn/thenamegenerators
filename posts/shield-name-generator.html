Famous shields

  • The Aegis was the shield (or goatskin cloak) of Zeus and Athena, later carrying the head of Medusa. Its name is why we still say "under the aegis of".
  • Svalinn, in Norse myth, stands in front of the sun. Without it, the mountains and seas would burn.
  • Pridwen is King Arthur's shield in Geoffrey of Monmouth, painted with the image of the Virgin Mary. In older Welsh tales the name belongs to his ship.
  • The shield of Achilles, made by the smith-god Hephaestus, gets a whole passage of the Iliad describing the scenes worked into it.

Reading a coat of arms

Heraldry has its own language, from Norman French. A coat of arms names the background (the field) first, then what's on it (the charge) and its colour:

  • Metals: or (gold) and argent (silver).
  • Colours: gules (red), azure (blue), vert (green), sable (black) and purpure (purple).
  • The rule of tincture: never put a colour on a colour or a metal on a metal, so the design can be seen from a distance. Every coat of arms the generator makes follows it.
  • A few words: a lion rampant stands on its hind legs; an eagle displayed has its wings spread; a mullet is a star.

So Gules, a lion rampant or means a gold lion standing up on a red shield: the arms Scotland's kings would recognise, with the colours swapped.

Naming a shield

  • A shield's job is to protect something. The "Name and duty" format makes that its title: Protector of the Pass, Protector of Lost Causes.
  • Let the arms tell the story. A family with a tower on its shield held a castle; a key might mean they kept a gate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are the coats of arms correct heraldry?

Yes, in their simple form: each gives a field and a charge in the proper terms, and follows the rule of tincture (no colour on colour, no metal on metal). Real arms can be much more complicated.

What does "gules" mean?

Red. Heraldry uses its own names for colours: gules (red), azure (blue), vert (green), sable (black), purpure (purple), and the metals or (gold) and argent (silver).
