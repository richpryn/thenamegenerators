Famous vampires and how they got their names

  • Lord Ruthven (John Polidori's The Vampyre, 1819) made the vampire an aristocrat. The name was borrowed from Lady Caroline Lamb's novel Glenarvon, where it belongs to a thinly disguised Lord Byron.
  • Carmilla (Sheridan Le Fanu, 1872) is really Countess Mircalla Karnstein, and also goes by Millarca: every alias is an anagram of her real name.
  • Count Dracula (Bram Stoker, 1897) takes his name from Vlad III of Wallachia, "Dracula", meaning son of Dracul. His father was called Dracul after the Order of the Dragon.
  • Count Orlok in the 1922 film Nosferatu exists because the film was an unauthorised adaptation of Dracula, so the names were changed.
  • Lestat de Lioncourt and Louis de Pointe du Lac (Anne Rice) carry the "de" of old French nobility.
  • Spike (Buffy the Vampire Slayer) was "William the Bloody" before that. Modern vampires often go by a nickname.

Three tricks vampire names use

  • Titles and noble particles. Count, Baron, Lady; "de", "von". Since Lord Ruthven, the vampire of fiction has usually been an aristocrat, living off everyone else. The With a title format adds one.
  • Anagrams and aliases. Someone who lives for centuries needs new identities. Carmilla shuffles her letters; "Alucard", Dracula backwards, has turned up from the 1943 film Son of Dracula to Castlevania and Hellsing.
  • Names of night and shadow. Latin and Greek words for night, evening and the underworld (Nox, Vesper, Erebus, Lethe) make strong single names for an old, nameless thing.

Choosing a style

A vampire usually keeps the name they were born with, so the style says when they were turned:

  • Gothic aristocrat: Western European nobility of the 1700s and 1800s, in the tradition of Dracula, Carmilla and Lestat. Pairs well with a title.
  • Old World: Romanian, Hungarian and Slavic names, for vampires from the lands where the folklore began.
  • Ancient: Roman, Greek and Near Eastern names, sometimes with a city instead of a surname: Lucius of Carthage.
  • Modern: ordinary names for vampires who blend in, as in Twilight or True Blood. The surnames hint at something (Graves, Frost, Locke) without giving the game away. Modern vampires don't use titles, so that format isn't offered.

Making the name your own

  • Decide when they were turned. A vampire born in 1750 and one born in 1990 shouldn't share a name style.
  • Give them a current alias. An old vampire living in the modern world might pair a gothic real name with a modern one, or use an anagram like Carmilla.
  • Add what others call them. An epithet like the Bloody or the Pale (try the Epithet Generator) is how mortals will remember them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are so many vampires counts?

Since Lord Ruthven in 1819, the vampire of fiction has usually been an aristocrat, and Count Dracula fixed the image for good. A title says old money, old blood and a castle, and that the vampire has been living off ordinary people for centuries.

What does the name Dracula mean?

"Son of Dracul". Vlad III of Wallachia was called Dracula because his father, Vlad II, was known as Dracul after joining the Order of the Dragon. Stoker borrowed the name for his count.

What is a good name for a female vampire?

For a classic gothic vampire, try an old European name with a title: Countess Elisabeth Ravenscourt, Lady Isadora Vane. For something older, a Roman or Greek name like Drusilla or Agrippina; for a modern vampire, an everyday name that blends in.

Do vampires change their names?

In a lot of fiction, yes: they live for centuries and need new identities. Carmilla's aliases are anagrams of her real name, and "Alucard" is Dracula backwards.
