How the Book Title Generator Works

It works from 1,514 titles and name parts, which combine into over 10,000 possible results.

The choices:

  • Genre: Any genre, Fantasy, Romance, Crime, Thriller, Horror, Sci-fi, Children's, YA, Literary, Historical.

Where the names come from: Every name is invented. Titles are built from word lists and title patterns for each genre: the cosy village murder, the woman in the window, the heir of somewhere, the summer at the seaside café. Every result is checked against a list of famous book titles, and franchise words (Hogwarts, Narnia, Jedi and so on) are left out. A title can't be owned, but search before you publish so yours stands out.

Each press gives you 8 names. "Show 8 more" adds 8 you haven't seen yet. Once you've seen them all, "Start again" shuffles the whole list again.

A title tells a browser what kind of book they're holding before they read a word of the blurb: a village murder, a dragon, a summer by the sea. This book title generator makes titles in ten genres, from fantasy and cosy crime to picture books and YA, using the shapes each genre's shelves are full of.

Every genre has its own title shape

Readers scan shelves and thumbnails fast, so genres settle into patterns that signal what's inside. The generator follows them:

  • Fantasy likes an object or person "of" an invented place, or two elements joined with "and". Real examples: Shadow and Bone (Leigh Bardugo) and A Court of Thorns and Roses (Sarah J. Maas).
  • Cosy crime names the crime and the setting: a village plus Murders, or "Death at" somewhere genteel. Richard Osman's The Thursday Murder Club is the best-known recent one.
  • Thrillers often put an ordinary person somewhere specific, as in The Girl on the Train (Paula Hawkins) and The Woman in the Window (A. J. Finn), or describe them with one unsettling word.
  • Romance sets a season at a cosy place, or names the trope: the deal, the bet, the fake date.
  • Children's books love a name and a thing (Charlie and the Chocolate Factory) or an animal who did something (The Tiger Who Came to Tea).
  • Literary fiction goes quieter: a family name, a small noun, or "What the river knew".

Those real titles are examples only; the generator checks its own results against a list of famous books and leaves them out.

Can someone else have the same title?

Yes, and it happens all the time. In the UK and the US a title on its own isn't protected by copyright, so two books can share one. A series name can be registered as a trade mark, though, and a title that copies a famous book will confuse readers and bury yours in search results.

Before you commit, search the title on Amazon, Goodreads and a library catalogue. If several books already use it, add a word or swap one: the generator's halves mix well. This is general information, not legal advice.

Tips for picking the one

  • Check it as a thumbnail. Most people first see a cover the size of a stamp, so short titles read best.
  • Say it out loud. Readers recommend books by saying the title, so it should be easy to say and spell.
  • Match the genre. A romance called The Pale Choir will find the wrong readers.
  • Keep a list. Generate a few batches, star the ones you like, then sleep on it. The one you still like in the morning is usually right.

Frequently Asked Questions

+How do I come up with a title for my book?
Choose your genre and generate a few batches, then mix halves of the ones you like. Titles that name a person, a place or a promise (a murder, a summer, a secret) tell readers fastest what they're getting.
+What is a good title for a fantasy book?
Something with a person or object and an invented place, like Heir of Embers or The Wardens of Highcairn. Choose Fantasy; the place names are made up, so they won't clash with anyone else's world.
+What is a good title for a children's book?
A name and an adventure works well, like Milo and the Runaway Rocket, or an animal who does something, like The Hedgehog Who Built a Rocket. Choose Children's for more.
+Can I use a book title that already exists?
Usually yes, as titles alone generally aren't protected by copyright in the UK or US. But a title shared with a well-known book will confuse readers, so search before you publish and pick one that stands out.
+Does it work for short stories and fan fiction?
Yes. Short stories suit the shorter titles, such as What the Water Took or Notes on Leaving. Choose Any genre for a mix, or the genre your story fits.
+Are these real book titles?
They're made up and checked against a list of famous books, so you won't get Gone Girl or The Hobbit. A smaller book may still share one by chance, which is why a quick search is worth it.
