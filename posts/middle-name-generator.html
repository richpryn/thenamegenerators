Where the names come from: These are real historical names. Every middle name is a real first name recorded by the Office for National Statistics (ONS) for babies born in England and Wales: its baby name counts for 1996 to 2025 and its top 100 lists every ten years from 1904, under the Open Government Licence v3.0. The ONS doesn't publish middle names, so the lists use first names, sorted by popularity, era, length and syllables. Names that are also everyday words, brands, place names or famous characters were left out, as were hyphenated names and anything rude.

Eight good middle names

All eight came from the generator, typed with Olivia Bennett or Oliver Hughes:

  • Olivia Mae Bennett: a one-syllable middle name gives a long first name and a two-syllable surname a beat to rest on.
  • Olivia Margaret Bennett: Margaret was the top girls' name in England and Wales in 1924, 1934 and 1944, so it's a natural way to honour a grandmother or great-grandmother.
  • Olivia Juniper Bennett: a nature name that's rarer than Rose or Ivy, tucked in the middle where it can be a little bolder.
  • Olivia Rose Elizabeth Bennett: two middle names, short then long, so the whole name still flows.
  • Oliver Jude Hughes: short and strong between two longer names, and the initials O. J. H. spell nothing.
  • Oliver James Hughes: one of the most lasting boys' names there is: it's in every one of the ONS's ten-yearly top 100 lists from 1904 to 2024.
  • Oliver Theodore Hughes: long and flowing, with a ready nickname (Teddy or Theo) if he wants one.
  • Oliver Arthur Hughes: an old name back in fashion. Arthur fell out of the top 100 after the 1950s and was fifth for boys in 2025.

How to make a middle name flow

  • Vary the syllables. Names of different lengths read more easily than names of the same length. Count them: Olivia (4) Mae (1) Bennett (2) has more rhythm than Olivia Amelia Bennett. With a one-syllable surname, a longer middle name helps: Oliver Theodore Ward.
  • Watch the joins. Say the whole name out loud. If one name ends with the sound the next begins with (Grace Simmons, Rose Sutton), the two can run together.
  • Avoid accidental rhymes and jingles. Jane Payne or Kate Waite is easy to miss on paper and hard to forget once heard.
  • Think about the same initial. A middle name starting with the same letter as the surname gives a touch of alliteration (Ivy Hope Hughes). It works once; three of the same sound is a lot.
  • Write out the initials. First, middle and surname initials go on monograms, school labels and email addresses, so check they don't spell a word you'd rather avoid: Paige Isla Gray gives P. I. G., and Frederick Albert Thomas gives F. A. T.

Where the middle names come from

The ONS counts the first names on every birth registered in England and Wales, but it doesn't publish middle names, so the generator draws on first names, which is where most middle names come from anyway. Each style is a different cut of the same records:

  • Popular now: the most popular names of 2025, such as Rose, Florence and Theodore.
  • 20th century: names that made the ONS top 100 at least once between 1904 and 1994, from Edith and Albert to Claire and Darren.
  • Short: four letters or fewer, from names well used since 1996: Mae, Ivy, Jude, Kit.
  • Long and flowing: three syllables or more, such as Penelope, Elizabeth and Sebastian.
  • Less common: names that have been given in England and Wales since 1996 but have never been near the top, like Marigold and Ambrose.

Names that are also everyday words, brands or famous characters were left out.

Choosing a middle name in practice

  • Use it to honour someone. The middle name is the traditional place for a grandparent's or godparent's name, or a family surname. It's also where you can keep a name you love but feel is too much as a first name.
  • Check the gender balance. Plenty of names work for either (Wren, Rowan, Ellis). Choose Girl or boy to see both.
  • Try two. Two middle names let you honour two people. Choose Two middle names and keep them different lengths.
  • Decide before you register. In England and Wales a birth must be registered within 42 days (21 days in Scotland), and the middle names go on the birth certificate with the first name.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What middle name goes with Olivia?
A short one usually works best after a long first name: Olivia Mae, Olivia Rose or Olivia Jane. Type Olivia and your surname, choose Short, and listen to the whole name.
+How do I choose a middle name?
Say the whole name aloud, mix long and short names, and check the initials. Type your first name and surname here and try a few styles to see what sounds right.
+What are good middle names for boys?
Short names like Jude, Kit and Max sit well after most first names, and longer ones like Theodore or Sebastian suit a short first name. Choose Boy, then Short or Long and flowing.
+What are good middle names for girls?
Rose, Mae and Grace are easy after a long first name; Elizabeth or Penelope suit a short one, like Eve Penelope. Choose Girl and a style, then type your names in.
+Can you have two middle names?
Yes, plenty of people do, often to honour two relatives. Choose Two middle names, and try one short and one long so the full name still flows.
+Where do these middle names come from?
From the Office for National Statistics' records of baby names in England and Wales, from 1904 to 2025. They're real first names, sorted into styles by popularity, era and length.
+When do I need to decide on a middle name?
By the time you register the birth: within 42 days in England and Wales, or 21 days in Scotland. The middle names go on the birth certificate.
