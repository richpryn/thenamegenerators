How the Fantasy City Name Generator Works

It works from 1,309 names and name parts, which combine into over 24,000 possible results.

The choices:

  • Name Format:
    • Name only, e.g. Skygate
    • With an epithet, e.g. Hoargate, the Hold of Winter

Each press gives you 8 names. "Show 8 more" adds 8 you haven't seen yet, until the names for the choices you've made run out.

A great city needs a name that can carry its reputation. This fantasy city name generator sorts names by the kind of city you're building: a shining capital, a fortress on the border, a port full of smugglers, a hold in the frozen north, the ruin of something older, or a market city on the river. Add an epithet and it comes with a reputation too: Ironwall, the Last Bastion.

How real cities got their names

  • New towns. Surprisingly many great cities were once just "the new city": Naples comes from the Greek Neapolis, Carthage from the Phoenician Qart-hadasht, Novgorod is Russian for "new town".
  • Founders. Alexander the Great named Alexandria after himself; Constantine renamed Byzantium Constantinople.
  • The land. A ford, a hill, a harbour, a river mouth. Most of the world's cities are named for where they stand.
  • Several names at once. Byzantium, Constantinople and Istanbul are the same city. A city that has changed hands keeps its old names in its streets and stories.

Six kinds of city

  • Shining capital: gold, white stone and towers. Goldspire, Lionhall.
  • Fortress city: walls first, people second. Ironwall, Ravenkeep.
  • Port city: harbours, lighthouses and quays. Saltreach, Gullquay.
  • Northern and frozen: stone halls under snow. Rimehelm, Wolfhearth.
  • Ancient and ruined: something greater was here once. Sunkenmere, Echodeep.
  • River and market city: bridges, barges and trade. Heronford, Copperbridge.

Giving a city an epithet

Real cities collect nicknames: Rome is the Eternal City, Paris the City of Light, and Venice was La Serenissima, the most serene. Fantasy does the same: Minas Tirith means "Tower of the Guard", and King's Landing is named for the place Aegon the Conqueror came ashore.

An epithet is a short way to tell the reader what a city is famous for, or what it wants you to think it is. Choose "With an epithet" and the generator adds one that suits the kind of city. A fun trick is to pair a name and epithet that don't quite agree: a city called the Unbroken that everyone knows was sacked twice.

Building a city around its name

  • Name the districts after the city's history. A port city has a Saltgate and an Old Harbour; a conquered city has a quarter still called by the old name.
  • Keep the sound of the country. A city should sound like the land around it. Pick a country style in the Fantasy Country Name Generator, then use a city name in the same spirit.
  • Scale down for towns. Smaller places sound humbler. For villages and market towns, the Fantasy Town Name Generator builds names from real English place-name endings.

Frequently Asked Questions

+Are these city names from any game or book?
No. Famous fantasy cities the parts could make by chance, like Ironforge, Winterhold and Neverwinter, are left out, so the names are free to use in your own world.
+What's the difference between a city name and a town name?
Grandeur. City names here use bigger words (spire, throne, bastion) and can carry an epithet, while the Fantasy Town Name Generator uses the humble endings of real villages and market towns, like -thorpe and -ley.
+Can I use these names in D&D or my book?
Yes, they're free to use and you don't need to credit the site.
