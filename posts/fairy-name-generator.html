Fairies and their names in folklore

  • Don't say their name. People in Britain and Ireland were careful how they spoke of fairies, calling them the Good Folk, the Fair Folk or the Good Neighbours, to stay on their right side.
  • A name is power. In the English tale Tom Tit Tot, the same story as Rumpelstiltskin, guessing the creature's name is the only way to escape the bargain.
  • The sídhe. In Ireland the fairy folk are the aos sí, the people of the mounds, living inside the hills.
  • Iron. Almost everywhere in folklore, cold iron drives fairies away. A horseshoe over the door did the job.

Four kinds of fairy

  • Flower fairies: tiny, and named after small green things, the way Shakespeare named Titania's attendants in A Midsummer Night's Dream: Peaseblossom, Cobweb, Moth and Mustardseed. These follow suit: Thistledown, Cloverbell, Sloeblossom.
  • The Seelie Court: Scottish folklore's "blessed" fairies, more likely to reward kindness than punish rudeness. Lords and ladies with long, graceful names.
  • The Unseelie Court: the other kind, who ride out with the Wild Hunt and take people who wander too far. Same graceful names, much worse epithets.
  • Household sprites: brownies, hobs and pixies who live in the house or farm and work at night. Their names are short and homely: Hob, Tib, Grig.

Famous fairy names

  • Oberon, king of the fairies, comes from Alberich, a dwarf of German legend, by way of the French Auberon. Titania, his queen, is a name Ovid uses for Diana.
  • Queen Mab, "the fairies' midwife", appears in Mercutio's famous speech in Romeo and Juliet.
  • Puck, or Robin Goodfellow, was a puck before he was Puck: the word meant a mischievous sprite.
  • Tinker Bell is named for her trade. J. M. Barrie wrote that she mends pots and kettles, a tinker's work.

Writing a fairy

  • Fairies keep their word exactly. Not the spirit of it, the letter. A fairy with a title like the Bargainer should be written that way.
  • Never thank them, in some traditions: it implies a debt. Your characters might learn that the hard way.
  • Give a household sprite a job. A brownie who's offended by a gift of clothes and leaves for good is a whole story in folklore.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What's the difference between the Seelie and Unseelie Courts?
They come from Scottish folklore. The Seelie ("blessed") Court are the kinder fairies, who may help those who treat them well; the Unseelie Court are hostile and dangerous. Both are perilous to cross.
+Are fairies and fae the same?
"Fae" is an old spelling of "fay", another word for fairy, and modern fantasy often uses it for the grand, dangerous fairies of the courts. The generator covers both.
+Can I use these names for D&D or a story?
Yes, they're free to use and you don't need to credit the site. They suit D&D's Feywild, eladrin and pixies as well as folklore-inspired stories.
