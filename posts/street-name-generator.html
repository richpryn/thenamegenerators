Good street names sound lived in: a local name and the right ending, like Rothwell Close for a quiet cul-de-sac or Vernon Mews for a row of converted stables. How real streets got their names

Old street names are practical. They say where the road goes, what was sold there or who lived on it:

  • High Street was the main street of a town, "high" meaning important rather than uphill.
  • Gate in York comes from the Old Norse gata, a street: Stonegate, Coppergate and Micklegate are streets, and the city's actual gateways are called bars.
  • The Shambles, also in York, is named after the benches butchers sold meat from. Pudding Lane in London, where the Great Fire of 1666 began, is said to be named after the offal, or "puddings", carted along it.
  • Trades gave streets their names all over Europe, which is why the fantasy style has Tilers' Passage and Apothecaries' Row.

The ending says what kind of street it is. A close is usually a cul-de-sac, a mews was a row of stables behind grand houses, a terrace is a row of joined houses, a row a line of cottages, and a wynd a narrow Scottish lane. American cities went another way: the 1811 plan for Manhattan laid out numbered streets and avenues, which is why the American style has East 93rd Street.

Choose With a description to give each street a detail, like Medlar Grove, lined with tiny Victorian cottages and cobbles.

Using street names

  • In a story or game, name the streets players will walk down, and let the rest be "the market" or "the docks".
  • Give each district a flavour: trade names near the market, grand mews by the palace, wynds and steps in the old town.
  • For a real development, UK street names are approved by the local council, usually after the developer has suggested them. Councils avoid names that are already used nearby.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What are good street names?
Names that sound lived in: a local or family name with the right ending, like Rothwell Close or Vernon Mews, or a trade name for a fantasy city, like Candlemakers' Alley. Choose a style to match the place.
+What's the difference between a close, a mews and a terrace?
A close is usually a cul-de-sac, a mews was originally a row of stables behind larger houses, and a terrace is a row of houses joined together.
+Who names new streets in the UK?
The local council approves street names, usually from suggestions made by the developer. Councils avoid duplicates and names that could be confusing for emergency services.
