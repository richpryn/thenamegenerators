How astronomers name galaxies

Our own galaxy is the Milky Way, and the word galaxy comes from the same idea: the Greek galaxias means "milky". Beyond that, very few galaxies have names at all.

  • Catalogue numbers do most of the work. In the 18th century Charles Messier listed fuzzy objects that astronomers might mistake for comets, so the Andromeda Galaxy is also M31. The New General Catalogue, or NGC, added thousands more.
  • Survey designations often include the object's position in the sky, which is why real codes look like a string of numbers with a plus or minus sign in the middle.
  • The few with names are named after constellations (Andromeda) or people: the Magellanic Clouds are named after Ferdinand Magellan, whose expedition described them.

Galaxies in your setting

  • Most stories need only one or two. Name your home galaxy and the one next door, and leave the rest as catalogue numbers.
  • Different people, different names. Scientists might use the code while the locals use a legend. That gap is a nice detail.
  • Distances are huge. Andromeda, our nearest large neighbour, is about 2.5 million light years away. Crossing between galaxies should feel like a very big deal.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What are good galaxy names for a sci-fi story?
A coined name with a shape word, like The Cyrenth Wheel or The Ambrevel Drift, or a catalogue code like VRD J1137+5508 for something more scientific. Choose Mythic or Poetic for a softer, older feel.
+How do real galaxies get their names?
Mostly with catalogue numbers, like M31 for the Andromeda Galaxy. Only a few have names, usually from a constellation or a person, like the Magellanic Clouds.
+Are any of these real galaxies?
No. The names and catalogue codes are invented, though the codes are built to look like real survey designations.
