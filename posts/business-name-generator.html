Turning a word into a business name

Single dictionary words are almost always registered by someone, somewhere. Building on them makes your name both available and memorable:

  • Word + what you do: Summit Plumbing, Ember Bakery. Clear and easy to find, but harder to protect.
  • Two words together: Emberlane, Wolfstone, Tideworks. Distinctive, and often easier to get as a domain.
  • Word + word: Ember & Oak, Velvet & Vine. Warm and crafted; popular with food, interiors and gifts.
  • Place + word: Severn Summit, Northgate Pixel. Local, and you're less likely to clash with a business elsewhere.
  • The word, a little changed: Summitly, Pixelry, Flowe. Brandable, but check people can spell it after hearing it once.

Check it's free before you fall for it

The names here are free to use, but that doesn't mean nobody else is using them. Before you commit, run through these checks:

  • Company register. In the UK, Companies House has a free company name availability checker. You can't register a name that's the "same as" an existing one, and some words (like Royal, Bank, British and Charity) need permission. In the US, check your state's business register.
  • Trade marks. Search the UK IPO, USPTO (US) or EUIPO (EU) trade mark databases. Trade marks are registered for particular goods and services, so a name taken by a software company might be fine for a bakery. Search the classes you'll trade in.
  • Domain and social handles. See if the .com (or .co.uk) and the handles you need are free. If not, a small change to the name is easier now than later.
  • A quick search. Search the name with your industry and town. If a business nearby does the same thing under a similar name, keep looking.

This is a starting point, not legal advice. If the name will carry a big investment, a trade mark attorney can check it properly.

What makes a name work

  • It passes the phone test. Can someone spell it after hearing it once?
  • It's short. One to three syllables fits on a sign, a van and an app icon.
  • It leaves room to grow. "Summit Cakes" is tied to cakes; "Summit Kitchen" can sell anything you bake or cook.
  • It's distinctive. Purely descriptive names ("Best Plumbing") are hard to remember and hard to protect as a trade mark.

Frequently Asked Questions

+Can I use these names for my business?
Yes, they're free to use and you don't need to credit the site. Just check the name you settle on isn't already taken or trademarked in your area and industry.
+How do I check a business name is available?
In the UK, use the Companies House name availability checker, then search the UK IPO trade mark database. In the US, check your state's business register and the USPTO trade mark search. Then check the domain and social handles.
+Why are the names single words?
Single words are the strongest starting point. Add what you do, a second word or a place, and you get a name that is both distinctive and more likely to be available.
+Should my business name say what I do?
It's a trade-off. A descriptive name tells customers what you do straight away but is harder to trade mark and to stand out with. An abstract name needs more explaining but is easier to own. Many businesses split the difference: a distinctive word plus a plain one, like Ember Bakery.
+Business, company or brand name generator: which should I use?
This one gives you strong single words to build a shop, trade or service name around. The Company Name Generator makes startup-style names: invented words, blends and -ify/-ly endings, and the Brand Name Generator suggests names for a product or range, sorted by industry and feel. Whichever you use, check the name is free before you commit to it.
