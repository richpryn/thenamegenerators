What makes an event name work

  • It says what it is. "Lantern Fields Festival" tells you more than "Lantern Fields". A plain word like Festival, Quiz or Live also helps people find the event in a search.
  • It sets the mood. "Prosecco and Pyjamas" and "Paintball and Pints" promise very different weekends.
  • It's easy to say and share. If someone can't tell a friend the name, or type it as a hashtag, it won't spread.
  • It fits on a poster. Two to four words reads at a glance on a flyer, a ticket or a phone screen.
  • It can grow. If it might become a yearly thing, choose a name that works with the year added: Lantern Fields 2027.

Ideas for each kind of event

  • Festivals and fairs: a place-like name plus the kind of event, like Foxglove Fayre or Golden Hour Weekender. "Fayre" suits village fetes and craft fairs; "Weekender" suits music over several days.
  • Gigs, club nights and open mics: record-collector words (Needle Drop, B-Side, Demo Tape) and alliteration with the night of the week (Tuned Up Tuesdays, Sonic Sundays) make a regular night easy to remember.
  • Fundraisers: name the activity or the cause, ideally both: Sleep Out for Shelter, Paws for Thought. People give more when they know what they're supporting.
  • Tournaments and gaming nights: a gaming word plus a format, like Respawn Invitational or Dice and Glory Cup. "Open" tells people anyone can enter.
  • Quiz nights: puns are expected (Quiz Pro Quo, Smarty Pints). Save the very best one for your team name.
  • Hen and stag dos: pick something the bride or groom will laugh at, and that's fine to see in the family group chat.
  • Work events: a forward-looking word plus a plain format (Groundwork Day, Common Ground Forum). Skip the jargon; people should know from the name whether to bring a laptop.

Before you announce it

The names here are free to use, but someone else may already run an event with the same one. A few quick checks save a reprint later:

  • Search the name with your town or area. Two events with the same name nearby confuse ticket buyers.
  • Check the ticket sites and socials. Search Eventbrite, Facebook events and the social platforms you'll use, and see whether the handle is free.
  • Check trade marks for anything that will grow. A festival or club night that becomes a brand is worth checking on the UK IPO or USPTO trade mark search.

That's a starting point, not legal advice. If the name will carry a big investment, ask a trade mark attorney.

Frequently Asked Questions

+How do I come up with a name for an event?
Start with what it is (festival, quiz, gig) and the mood you want, then try a name that says both in two to four words. Say it out loud and imagine it on a poster; if it works there, check nobody nearby is already using it.
+Should I put the year in my event name?
Add it to the edition, not the name: call the event "Lantern Fields Festival" and the poster "Lantern Fields Festival 2027". The name then works every year.
+What is a good name for a quiz night?
Puns on quiz, brains and pints work well: Quiz Pro Quo, Smarty Pints, Brains and Brews. Add the cause if it's for charity ("The Charity Quiz for the Village Hall").
+Can I use these names for a paid or commercial event?
Yes, they're free to use and you don't need to credit the site. Just check the name isn't already used by another event near you, and check trade marks if the event will become a brand.
