English Place-Name Endings and What They Mean

At a glance

Most English place-names began as plain descriptions made of one or two words: a feature of the land, a person, or a kind of settlement. The endings come mainly from Old English (such as -ton, -ham, -ley and -ford), from Old Norse in the north and east (-by, -thorpe, -thwaite) and, in a few cases, from Brittonic, the Celtic language spoken before English. A name's meaning has to be worked out from its earliest recorded spellings, not from its modern form.

The commonest Old English endings

Most English place-names were coined by speakers of Old English between the fifth and eleventh centuries, and the same handful of words turn up again and again [1][3]:

  • -ton (Old English tūn): an enclosure, then a farmstead, estate or village. It is the most frequent ending of all.
  • -ham (hām): a homestead or village. A second word, hamm, meant land in a river bend or hemmed in by water, and the two look identical today, so only early spellings can tell them apart [5].
  • -ley, -leigh (lēah): woodland, and later a clearing in it, as in Bromley, 'broom clearing'.
  • -bury, -borough (burh): a fortified place, from Iron Age hillforts to Alfred's fortified towns.
  • -ford: a river crossing, as in Oxford, 'the ford used by oxen'.
  • -wick, -wich (wīc, borrowed from Latin vīcus): a specialised settlement, such as a dairy farm, a trading place or a salt-works. Woolwich is usually explained as a place where wool was traded or shipped, although no record of such a trade survives.
  • -worth (worth): an enclosure, and -stead (stede): a place or site. Hampstead is simply hām-stede, 'homestead'.

Many names add a group: Birmingham is the homestead of Beorma's people, with -inga- meaning 'the people of' [2]. Names that end in -ing or -ings, such as Reading ('Rēada's people') and Hastings ('Hæsta's people'), began as the names of groups and were later used for the places where those groups lived [1].

The first part of a name is usually the interesting one: a person (Beorma, Rēada), an animal (oxen at Oxford), a plant (broom at Bromley) or a landmark. The ending just says what kind of place it was.

Norse names and the Danelaw

After Scandinavian settlement in the ninth century, Old Norse speakers named and renamed places across the north and east of England, the area later called the Danelaw [3]. Their commonest words were:

  • -by (bý): a farmstead or village, as in Grimsby, Grímr's farm [4], and Ashby, the farm by the ash trees.
  • -thorpe (thorp): an outlying or secondary farm, as in Scunthorpe, Skúma's outlying farm.
  • -thwaite (thveit): a clearing or meadow, very common in Cumbria, as in Braithwaite, 'broad clearing'.
  • -toft (topt): the plot of a house, as in Lowestoft.

The concentration of -by and -thorpe names in Lincolnshire, Yorkshire and the East Midlands is one of the clearest maps of where Scandinavian settlers made their homes [3].

Roman forts and older British names

The Latin word castra, a military camp, was borrowed into Old English as ceaster and used for the remains of Roman sites. It survives as -chester in the south, -caster in the north and east, and -cester in names like Leicester. Lancaster is the Roman fort on the River Lune [1].

Some names are older still, from Brittonic, the Celtic language of Roman Britain. River names are the most likely to survive: Avon is the Brittonic word for 'river', so River Avon really does mean 'river river' [2]. Pen ('head, end, hill') survives in names such as Penrith, and cumb, a valley, passed into English as -combe.

A few names can't be explained with confidence. Leeds goes back to Loidis, a district named by Bede in the eighth century. It is clearly pre-English, but scholars have proposed several origins, and none is certain [6].

Myths to be wary of

  • Guessing from the modern spelling. Names change over a thousand years. The ending -ham can be hām or hamm, so dictionaries always start from the earliest written forms [5].
  • Founder legends. Grimsby has a medieval story of a fisherman called Grim who founded the town, but the name only tells us that a man called Grímr, a common Norse name, once owned the farm [4].
  • Every -wich is a salt town. The Cheshire salt towns (Northwich, Middlewich, Nantwich) give the ending that reputation, but wīc was used for many kinds of specialised settlement.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What does -ton mean at the end of a place name?
It comes from Old English tūn, first an enclosure, then a farmstead, estate or village. It is the most common English place-name ending.
+What does -by mean in English place names?
It is Old Norse bý, a farm or village. Names ending in -by, like Grimsby and Ashby, are concentrated in the parts of England settled by Scandinavians.
+Why do so many towns end in -chester?
Old English speakers borrowed the Latin castra, a military camp, as ceaster and used it for the sites of Roman forts and towns. It survives as -chester, -caster and -cester.
