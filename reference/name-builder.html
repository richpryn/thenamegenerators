How names are built

Name Builder

At a glance

Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, Franks and Greeks made many names by joining two meaningful words: Æthelwulf is 'noble' + 'wolf', Theodorus is 'god' + 'gift'. How the builder works

Early Germanic names usually had two parts, and the second part showed whether the name was a man's or a woman's: Old English -wulf and -stan for men, -gifu and -hild for women. Greek names worked the same way with endings such as -dorus and -dora. The builder keeps to those patterns, uses only parts from the language you choose, and never repeats a part in one name.

Place names join a description to a word for a farm, a hill, a ford or a valley, so Old English æsc 'ash tree' and ford give a name like Ashford. Many of the results are, or sound like, real places; check before you use one in a story set in the real world.

Parents didn't usually pick names for their literal meaning: they passed parts down families, so a boy called Wulfstan wasn't thought of as a 'wolf stone'. The meanings tell you what the parts are, not what anyone intended.

Old Norse: Shown in the plain English spelling of the sagas in translation, without accents or the -r ending: Thorstein for Þorsteinn.

Old High German and Frankish: Shown in the forms that came into English and German through the Normans and Franks: Wilhelm, Gerhard, Ludwig.

Ancient Greek: Shown in the Latin spelling used in English: Theodorus, Nicolaus, Philippus.
