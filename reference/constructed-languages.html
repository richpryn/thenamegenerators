Constructed Languages and Naming Languages

At a glance

A constructed language, or conlang, is a language someone has invented on purpose. Famous examples for fiction include Tolkien's Elvish languages Quenya and Sindarin, Marc Okrand's Klingon for Star Trek, David J. Peterson's Dothraki and High Valyrian for HBO's Game of Thrones, and Paul Frommer's Na'vi for Avatar. Esperanto, published by L. L. Zamenhof in 1887, was built for a different job: to be an easy second language for real people. Many writers need only a naming language, a consistent set of sounds and a few word roots, rather than a full grammar.

Tolkien: the languages came first

Tolkien's Elvish languages are the best-known conlangs in fiction. Quenya (High-elven) and Sindarin (Grey-elven) are the two main ones, and most of the non-English names of people and places in his books come from them.[1] He had been building them for nearly forty years before The Fellowship of the Ring appeared in 1954, and he wrote that the stories were made to give the languages a world, rather than the other way round.[1]

His tastes shaped the sounds. In a 1955 letter to W. H. Auden he described a lifelong fascination with Welsh names and the thrill of finding a Finnish grammar, which made his own language heavily Finnish in sound and structure.[2] He also gave the Elvish languages invented histories, with older forms changing into newer ones, just as Old English became modern English.[1]

In a 1931 lecture, published as 'A Secret Vice', Tolkien argued that the urge to invent languages is common and is a legitimate part of the drive towards fantasy.[3]

Klingon, Dothraki and Na'vi

Klingon. Klingons in early Star Trek spoke English. For the third film the producers asked the linguist Marc Okrand to create a real language. He did not base it on any one language; its sounds all occur in human languages, but the combination is unusual. The Klingon Dictionary came out in 1985.[4]

Dothraki and High Valyrian. George R. R. Martin has said he is no linguist. He used only a handful of Dothraki words, such as khal and arakh, and made up only a few words of High Valyrian. When HBO filmed the series, the producers asked the Language Creation Society, which chose David J. Peterson's proposal. Peterson kept to the evidence in the books, such as word order and adjectives after nouns, and built a full working language from them.[5]

Na'vi. Paul Frommer, a professor at the University of Southern California's Marshall School of Business, created Na'vi for James Cameron's Avatar and still publishes new words and grammar notes on his blog.[6]

Esperanto: a different goal

Esperanto is a useful contrast. L. L. Zamenhof published it in 1887 in the hope that a shared second language would help different peoples understand each other. It was not meant to replace anyone's first language. It has no grammatical gender and no irregular verbs, and is spelt as it sounds.[7]

A language for fiction has the opposite aim. It should feel foreign and fit one imagined culture, as Klingon was meant to sound as alien as the Klingons looked while staying pronounceable for actors.[4] Esperanto, by contrast, draws most of its vocabulary from European languages Zamenhof knew well, such as Latin, French, German and Russian.[7]

Naming language or full language?

A full conlang has sounds, grammar, a large vocabulary and often a writing system. Most stories never need that. A naming language is a smaller kit: a set of sounds, rules for how syllables fit together, and a stock of roots and endings. Mark Rosenfelder's Language Construction Kit uses the term and notes that tools such as prefixes, suffixes and compounds are useful 'even if you want nothing more than a naming language'.[8]

Martin's books show this in practice. A few consistent words gave Dothraki a flavour on the page, and the full language was only needed when actors had to speak it.[5]

Rosenfelder also warns that working backwards, inventing words as needed and a grammar afterwards, leads to inconsistency.[8]

Building a simple naming language

This is writing advice based on the sources above, not a rule:

  • Pick the sounds first. Rosenfelder suggests working on the sounds before the spelling, then spelling them simply.[8]
  • Make a short list of roots with meanings, such as our invented tal 'stone' and ren 'river'.
  • Combine them by fixed rules, the way real place-names put a describing word before a word for the kind of place.
  • Write the rules down so later names follow them.

Names built this way, like our Talren and Renmara, sound related because they share roots and sounds, even if readers never learn what they mean.

Frequently Asked Questions

+Is Dothraki a real language?
It is a real constructed language. George R. R. Martin used only a few Dothraki words in his novels, and David J. Peterson expanded it for HBO's Game of Thrones into a full language with its own grammar and vocabulary. It has no native speakers.
+How did Tolkien create Elvish?
Over decades, starting before he wrote the stories. He built Quenya and Sindarin with their own sounds, grammar and invented histories, drawing on languages he loved, especially Finnish and Welsh, and kept revising them for the rest of his life.
+What is a naming language?
A small invented language used only for names: a fixed set of sounds, rules for building syllables, and a list of roots and endings. It gives a culture's names a consistent feel without the work of a full grammar.
