Naming Fictional Characters: How Writers Have Done It

At a glance

A charactonym is a character's name that hints at their nature, like Dickens's Gradgrind, the fact-grinding schoolmaster of Hard Times. Dickens kept notebooks of names, some copied from official lists of schoolchildren, and J. K. Rowling has said she collects names from maps, saints, war memorials and gravestones as well as inventing them. Tolkien took most of the dwarf names in The Hobbit, and the name Gandalf, from a list of dwarfs in the Old Norse poem Völuspá. Writers also need to take care with names that match real people: in a 1909 libel case a newspaper lost although it said its 'Artemus Jones' was made up.

Telling names

A name that hints at a character's nature is called a charactonym. Merriam-Webster's examples are Mistress Quickly and Caspar Milquetoast.[1] John Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress is full of them, such as Mr Talkative and Mr Worldly Wiseman.[2] A related word, aptronym, is used for a real person whose name suits them, often their job. It is sometimes said to have been coined by the American columnist Franklin P. Adams, but that is not certain.[2]

Dickens used telling names freely. In Hard Times the schoolmaster Thomas Gradgrind demands 'Facts' and nothing else, the teacher who serves his system is Mr M'Choakumchild, and two of Gradgrind's younger children are named Adam Smith and Malthus, after the economists.[3]

Dickens's notebooks of names

Dickens collected names as raw material. His friend and biographer John Forster published extracts from Dickens's Book of Memoranda, kept from 1855 to 1865. It includes lists of girls' and boys' first names that Dickens noted as coming from Privy Council Education lists, such as Rubina, Pleasant, Doctor, Zerubbabel and Pickles, then lists he seems to have made up himself, and a long run of 'available names'.[4]

Some went straight into the novels. 'Bradley' is on the boys' list and 'Amon Headston' among the available names; the schoolmaster in Our Mutual Friend is Bradley Headstone.[4][5]

Not every origin story holds up. A popular tale says Dickens saw a gravestone in Edinburgh's Canongate kirkyard for Ebenezer Lennox Scroggie, a 'meal man', misread it as 'mean man', and invented Scrooge. The story is widely repeated, but it rests on later retellings rather than anything Dickens is known to have written, and the stone itself is said to be long gone. Treat it as a good story, not a fact.[6]

Rowling's sources, in her own words

J. K. Rowling has described a mix of collecting and inventing. In 1999 she said she keeps notebooks of unusual names, gathered from saints, place-names, war memorials and gravestones, while names such as Quidditch and Malfoy were made up.[7] In another interview that year she added maps, street names and people she met, said Voldemort was invented, and said Mrs Norris comes from Jane Austen.[8]

Some individual stories she has told:

  • Dumbledore is an old word for a bumblebee, chosen because she pictured him humming to himself.[7] Dictionaries record it as an archaic or dialect word, used by writers such as Thomas Hardy.[12]
  • Potter was the surname of a family she lived near as a child.[9][7]
  • Gilderoy Lockhart joins a highwayman from the Dictionary of Phrase and Fable with a surname from a First World War memorial.[10]
  • She says she named the four Hogwarts houses on the back of an aeroplane sick bag.[11]

You will often read that Snape was named after the village of Snape in Suffolk. We could not trace that to an interview we could check, so treat it as unconfirmed.

Tolkien's Norse dwarfs and English kings

The Old Norse poem Völuspá contains a list of dwarfs, the Dvergatal. It names Durinn, Dvalinn, Dáinn, Bívurr, Bávurr, Bömburr, Nóri, Óinn, Þorinn, Þráinn, Fíli, Kíli, Glóinn, Dóri and Óri, and also Gandalfr and Eikinskjaldi.[13] Readers of The Hobbit will recognise most of Thorin's company, Gandalf, and Thorin's byname Oakenshield.

The meanings are less clear than the borrowing. Gandalfr combines gandr, a word whose exact sense is uncertain, with álfr, 'elf'.[14] Eikinskjaldi is usually read as 'oak-shield', but eikinn can also mean 'savage'.[15]

In Appendix F of The Lord of the Rings, Tolkien explains that he represented the languages of Men in the north with Old Norse and the language of Rohan with Old English, and that the Dwarves used names from the Men they lived among.[16] So the king of Rohan is Théoden, from Old English þēoden, a poetic word for a prince or king.[17]

Avoiding names too close to real people

In 1909 the House of Lords upheld a libel award to Thomas Artemus Jones, a barrister. A newspaper had published a scandalous story about an 'Artemus Jones' it said was imaginary, but friends of the real man believed it was about him.[18][19] The law has developed since, and this is not legal advice, but the case shows why writers check names.

Practical steps, as advice: search the full name together with the character's job and town; be most careful with unusual names and with villains; and in fantasy, prefer invented or historical forms over the names of living people.

Frequently Asked Questions

+Where did J.K. Rowling get the names for Harry Potter?
By her own account, from a mix of sources: notebooks of names collected from saints, place-names, maps, war memorials and gravestones, plus some she invented, such as Voldemort, Malfoy and Quidditch. Dumbledore is an old word for a bumblebee, and Potter was the surname of childhood neighbours.
+Where did Dickens get the name Ebenezer Scrooge?
No one knows for certain. A popular story says Dickens misread an Edinburgh gravestone for Ebenezer Scroggie, 'meal man', as 'mean man', but it rests on later retellings rather than anything Dickens wrote, so treat it as a legend.
+Where do Tolkien's dwarf names come from?
From the Dvergatal, a list of dwarf names in the Old Norse poem Völuspá. It includes Durinn, Þorinn, Fíli, Kíli, Glóinn, Bömburr, Óinn and Gandalfr, as well as Eikinskjaldi, behind Thorin's byname Oakenshield.
