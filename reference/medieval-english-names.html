Names through history

Medieval English Names

At a glance

After the Norman Conquest of 1066, English families gradually swapped Old English names for ones the Normans brought, such as William, Robert and Richard, and for saints' names like John. By the 1200s a handful of names covered a large share of men, so people told each other apart with pet forms such as Hob, Dick and Wilkin. Many of those pet forms later became surnames: Hobbs, Dixon, Wilkins.

What the Normans changed

Before 1066 most English people had Old English names built from two parts, like Æthelred or Wulfstan. The Normans brought a different stock: names of Continental Germanic origin such as William, Robert, Richard, Ralph, Walter, Roger and Hugh.[1] Over the next few generations English families took these up too, first among landholders and then much more widely.

The change wasn't overnight, and historians still debate how much was the Conquest itself and how much was fashion and the growing cult of the saints. Alongside the Norman names came saints' names such as John, Thomas, Nicholas and Peter. A few Old English names survived, mostly where a royal saint kept them alive: Edward, after Edward the Confessor, and Edmund, after the martyred king of East Anglia.[1]

A very small pool of names

By the 1200s the range of men's names had narrowed sharply. In the Fine Rolls of Henry III, William accounts for roughly one man in seven, and William, John, Richard and Robert together for over a third.[5] Studies of other records give somewhat different figures, so treat the exact percentages as approximate, but the pattern is the same everywhere: a village could have several Williams and Johns at once.

That is one reason surnames became necessary in this period. A second name, whether a place, a job, a father's name or a nickname, told the Williams apart.[2]

Women's names

Women's names followed the same path. Old English names such as Æthelflæd and Eadgifu gave way to names the Normans favoured, and to saints' names. Alice, Agnes, Joan, Matilda, Isabel, Emma and Margaret were among the most common, with Alice near the top of many lists.[5] Matilda was usually said, and often written, as Maud, and Joan grew in popularity as the feminine of John.[1]

Women's names took pet forms too, and some became surnames in the same way. Emmott, from Emma with the French ending -ot, is one. Others passed to children as mothers' names, which is why a few English surnames come from women's names rather than men's.[2] Records of women are thinner than records of men, so counts of women's names are less certain.

Pet forms and rhyming nicknames

In everyday speech people rarely used the full name. They shortened it, and often changed the first sound to make a rhyme: Robert gave Rob, Hob and Dob; Richard gave Dick and Hick; Roger gave Hodge.[2][4] They also added little endings that worked like '-y' or '-ie' today:

  • -kin, probably from Flemish or Low German: Wilkin, Watkin (Walter), Jenkin (John).
  • -cock, of uncertain origin: Wilcock, Hancock.
  • -ot and -et, from French: Elliot, Emmott.
  • -in, also from French: Robin, from Rob.

Many of these pet forms became surnames, often with -s or -son added: Hobbs, Dixon, Hodgson, Wilkins, Watkins, Jenkins, Wilcox, Robinson.[2] Some popular stories go further than the evidence. 'Bill' for William, for example, is usually dated to long after the Middle Ages.[1]

Names in the records

Most of what we know comes from written records, and those were usually in Latin. A clerk writing a charter, a court roll or a tax list turned William into Willelmus, John into Johannes and Richard into Ricardus, even when everyone in the village said Will, Jack or Dick.[3] Pet forms do turn up, especially in later court rolls and as surnames, which is how scholars can date them.

If you're naming a medieval character, mix a common baptismal name with a byname a neighbour would actually use: William the smith, Hob of the mill, Wat son of Roger. That is closer to how people were known than a long, grand name.

Frequently Asked Questions

+What were common names in medieval England?
For men, William was the most common name by the 1200s, followed by John, Richard and Robert. Others included Thomas, Walter, Roger, Hugh and Henry. These Norman and saints' names largely replaced Old English ones after 1066.
+Why is Bob short for Robert?
Medieval English often made pet names by shortening a name and changing its first sound to rhyme: Robert gave Rob, Hob and Dob. Bob follows the same pattern, though it's recorded later than Hob and Dob.
+Where do surnames like Wilkins and Hancock come from?
From medieval pet names. Wilkin is Will plus the ending -kin, and Hancock is Han, a pet form of John, plus -cock. Adding -s or -son turned them into family names.
