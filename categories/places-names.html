Place Name Generators

Towns, cities and countries for fantasy worlds and maps, plus café names.

Real place names are built from parts that once meant something: -ford was a river crossing, -wick a settlement, -dale a valley. Fantasy names feel real when they follow the same logic, so a mining town sounds like a mining town.

Start with the Fantasy Town Name Generator, which sorts names by what the town does, then zoom out with cities and countries. Naming a real place? The Café Name Generator is here too.

How to approach place names

Real place names started out as plain descriptions: who lived there, what stood there, or what you'd find when you arrived. Oxford was the ford where oxen crossed; Ashby was the farm by the ash trees. Over centuries the words wore smooth, which is why they sound natural rather than invented. The best fantasy places work the same way. Decide what the place is known for, choose an old-sounding word for it, and add an ending that says what kind of place it is: a ford, a haven, a hold, a mere.

Quick tips

  • Name a place after what's there.
  • Give each region its own language.
  • Let old names sound worn down.
  • Say it out loud before you use it.
  • Search it, in case it's a real town.

Examples

Ebonwold

KINGDOM

A dark kingdom you can picture before you see the map.

Combines 'ebon' (black, as in ebony) with 'wold', an Old English word for forest or high ground. A kingdom of black woods, in two syllables.

Ferryhaven

CITY

A river port that sounds like it has been trading for centuries.

Combines 'ferry' (a river crossing) with 'haven' (a safe harbour). Both are plain English words, so it reads like a real place that grew up around its trade.

Candleworth

VILLAGE

A craft village you can smell from the road.

Combines 'candle' with '-worth', an Old English word for an enclosed settlement, as in Tamworth. It sounds like the village where the chandlers lived, which is exactly how real names began.

Llyn y Gloch

LANDMARK

A lake that comes with a legend attached.

Welsh for 'lake of the bell'. A bell in a lake suggests a drowned church or a sunken village, the kind of story real Welsh lakes are known for.
