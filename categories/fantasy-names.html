Fantasy characters and creatures

Fantasy Name Generators

Elves, dwarves, orcs, dragons, wizards, demons and more, for D&D, stories and games.

Fantasy names work best when they follow the rules of their world. An elf from Tolkien sounds different from a D&D elf, and a Skyrim orc is named nothing like a Warcraft one. These generators follow those traditions, and each page explains how names in that world work, so you can bend the rules on purpose.

Most characters need more than a name. Add an epithet (the Bold, Dragonslayer) with the Epithet Generator, a hometown from the place name generators and a weapon from the weapon and item generators.

How to approach fantasy names

The best fantasy names sound as if they belong to a language, even an invented one. Tolkien built his elves' names from languages he made up, and took most of his dwarves' names from the Old Norse poem the Völuspá, which is why they still sound right together. Decide how each people sounds before you name anyone: soft and flowing for elves, hard and blunt for dwarves and orcs, grand and ancient for dragons. Then keep to it, so a reader can tell where a character comes from before they're introduced.

Quick tips

  • Give each people its own sound.
  • Keep names easy to read aloud.
  • Add an epithet for a reputation.
  • Avoid famous names from books and games.
  • Vary the length across a cast.

Examples

Nervor Flintmantle

DWARF

A dwarf you can picture at the forge.

Flint and mantle are both plain English words, one hard and one heavy, which is the way dwarven surnames tend to work: stone, metal and things you'd make from them.

Glacarax, the Long Winter

DRAGON

An ice dragon whose name sounds like cracking.

Glac- comes from the Latin for ice, as in glacier, and the hard -arax ending gives it a growl. The epithet tells you what happens when it wakes.

Lethandor

ELF

A flowing elven name with a sense of place.

Soft consonants and three easy syllables make it sound elvish, and the -dor ending echoes Tolkien's Elvish, where it means land.

Riverlock, the Mill City

CITY

A working city with its trade in its name.

A river lock and a mill are things you'd actually find there, and the epithet works like a real nickname, the way Manchester was called Cottonopolis.
